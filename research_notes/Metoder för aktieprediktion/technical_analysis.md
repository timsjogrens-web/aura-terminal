# Classic Technical Analysis and Chart-Pattern Trading for US Equities

## What techniques do retail traders/finance-content creators commonly use, and what data does each require (free vs. paid)?

### Takeaway
Retail/YouTube technical analysis is built from a fairly small, standard toolkit — moving averages/crossovers, MACD, RSI, Bollinger Bands, support/resistance, classic chart patterns (head-and-shoulders, double top/bottom), candlestick patterns, Fibonacci retracements, and volume indicators (OBV) — and every one of them can be computed from plain daily OHLCV (open/high/low/close/volume) price-and-volume history, which is available for free for US equities (e.g., via `yfinance`/Yahoo Finance's unofficial v8 chart endpoint, or Finnhub's free tier). No paid feed is required for any of the classic techniques; paid data only becomes relevant for higher-frequency (intraday/tick), deeper history, or redundancy/reliability needs.

### Cited Findings
- Yahoo Finance shut down its official public API in 2017, but the `yfinance` library and the underlying v8 "chart" endpoint it scrapes remain free, with no paid tier, and return decades of historical OHLCV data at intervals from 1-minute to monthly for most tickers — [Yahoo Finance API Guide](https://scrapfly.io/blog/posts/guide-to-yahoo-finance-api)
- Finnhub's free tier provides real-time quotes, historical OHLCV candles, company profiles, and other fundamentals at 60 API calls/minute, with no cost — [Top 5 Free Financial Data APIs](https://dev.to/williamsmithh/top-5-free-financial-data-apis-for-building-a-powerful-stock-portfolio-tracker-4dhj)
- All of the named indicators (moving-average crossovers, MACD, RSI, Bollinger Bands, OBV) are mathematical transformations of close price and/or volume series — no separate order-book, fundamentals, or alternative data is needed to compute them (inference from indicator definitions encountered across the methodology studies below, e.g. Bollinger Bands/RSI/MACD studies on daily close-price data — [Popularity versus Profitability: Evidence from Bollinger Bands](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318717250_Popularity_versus_Profitability_Evidence_from_Bollinger_Bands))
- Chart-pattern recognition (head-and-shoulders, double tops, etc.) in the primary academic methodology (Lo, Mamaysky & Wang) is performed with kernel regression smoothing applied directly to the daily closing-price series of CRSP (daily OHLC-type) stock data — no additional data source — [Foundations of Technical Analysis (NBER working paper)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w7613/w7613.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because every classic TA technique only needs daily (or intraday) OHLCV, a personal stock-signal dashboard could implement the entire retail TA toolkit on free data sources alone; the barrier to adding these signals is computational/implementation effort, not data cost or access.
- Free-tier rate limits (e.g., Finnhub's 60 calls/minute) are the more likely practical constraint for a multi-ticker dashboard than the cost of the data itself.

### Gaps
- No single source enumerating the complete "most common" list used specifically by retail/YouTube finance creators was found; the list above is inferred from what is both commonly named across the indicator/pattern-efficacy literature and self-evident to require only OHLCV. No independent survey/poll of retail-trader-content tool usage was located.

## What does credible academic research say about whether technical-analysis signals have real, replicated predictive power on US equities, after transaction costs?

### Takeaway
The most-cited rigorous studies find technical analysis has, at best, weak and inconsistent predictive power on US equities: Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000) found some chart patterns carry statistically significant information (especially on Nasdaq) but explicitly note that statistical significance is not the same as trading profitability after transaction costs; Sullivan, Timmermann & White's (1999) bootstrap "Reality Check" found that the best technical rule in the in-sample Dow Jones period failed to outperform out-of-sample and showed no edge on S&P 500 futures once data-snooping bias was corrected for; and Park & Irwin's (2007) survey of 95 "modern" studies found only 56 reporting positive profitability, with nearly all suffering from data-snooping, selection bias, or unreliable transaction-cost/risk estimation — and crucially found early studies showed TA profitable in FX/futures but *not* in stock markets specifically.

### Cited Findings
- Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000), "Foundations of Technical Analysis," used nonparametric kernel regression to automatically detect 10 chart patterns across 350 NYSE/AMEX and 350 Nasdaq stocks, 1962–1996; comparing conditional vs. unconditional return distributions, they found "some technical indicators do provide incremental information and may have some practical value," with all 10 patterns statistically significant for Nasdaq stocks vs. only 5 of 10 for NYSE/AMEX — [NBER Working Paper 7613](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w7613/w7613.pdf); [Journal of Finance, 2000](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/0022-1082.00265)
- Lo, Mamaysky & Wang explicitly caution that finding a pattern "statistically informative" does not mean it yields a profitable trading strategy once transaction costs are factored in — [NBER Working Paper 7613](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w7613/w7613.pdf)
- Sullivan, Timmermann & White (1999), "Data-Snooping, Technical Trading Rule Performance, and the Bootstrap" (Journal of Finance), tested an expanded universe of ~7,846 technical rules (building on Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron) on 100 years of Dow Jones daily data using White's Reality Check bootstrap to correct for data-snooping bias; the best rule beat the benchmark in the original in-sample period even after correcting for data snooping, but **failed to outperform in the subsequent 10-year out-of-sample period**, and when applied to S&P 500 futures there was **no evidence the best rule outperformed** once data-snooping was accounted for — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=65140); [Journal of Finance abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/0022-1082.00163)
- Park & Irwin (2007), "What Do We Know About the Profitability of Technical Analysis?" (Journal of Economic Surveys), reviewed 95 "modern" empirical studies: 56 found positive/profitable results, 20 found negative results, 19 found mixed results; but the paper stresses that "most empirical studies are subject to various problems in their testing procedures, e.g. data snooping, ex post selection of trading rules..., and difficulties in estimation of risk and transaction costs" — [Journal of Economic Surveys abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-6419.2007.00519.x)
- Park & Irwin also note that earlier-generation studies found technical trading profitable in **foreign exchange and futures markets, but not in stock markets** specifically — a finding directly relevant to equities — (per search synthesis of the Park & Irwin survey) [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/4765054_What_do_we_know_about_profitability_of_technical_analysis)
- On Bollinger Bands specifically: a study ("Popularity versus Profitability: Evidence from Bollinger Bands") found that trading on Bollinger Bands was very profitable before their 1983 introduction, but profitability decreased after publication/popularization (especially after John Bollinger's 2001 book), and **after adjusting for transaction costs, Bollinger Bands are consistently unable to earn profits in excess of buy-and-hold** — [Popularity versus Profitability: Evidence from Bollinger Bands](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318717250_Popularity_versus_Profitability_Evidence_from_Bollinger_Bands)
- A broader RSI/MACD study found that "all technical indicators perform poorly in terms of profitability and predictive power" in at least one tested setting, while a different MACD/RSI study on the London FT30 index did find several statistically significant and profitable strategy variants assuming a 0.5% transaction fee — a direct contradiction across studies that should be flagged as unresolved — [An Empirical Analysis of the Profitability of Technical Indicators](https://lup.lub.lu.se/luur/download?func=downloadFile&recordOId=8905915&fileOId=8905916)
- Moving-average crossover studies show the dual moving-average crossover (DMAC) strategy "can provide steady profits when no slippage is assumed," but "when slippage is accounted for... over 65% of the possible DMAC combinations" become unprofitable, and MA strategies become less profitable when implemented via ETFs vs. the underlying index — [Duke University DMAC analysis](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Teaching/BA453_2002/CCAM/CCAM.htm); [Testing moving average trading strategies on ETFs](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927539819300830)
- Head-and-shoulders patterns: a study on S&P 500 and Russell 2000 stocks (1990–1999) found "strong evidence that the head-and-shoulders pattern had power to predict excess returns" with risk-adjusted excess returns of 7–9%/year in some cuts, BUT a trading strategy that shorts S&P 500 stocks on H&S signals alone "is not profitable over 1990–1999," with only weak evidence of profitability for Russell 2000 stocks — because the market's upward drift overwhelmed the pattern's modest underperformance signal — [The Predictive Power of Head-and-Shoulders Price Patterns in the US Stock Market](https://www.biz.uiowa.edu/faculty/gsavin/papers/hsrevision_paw_10%2019%2006.pdf)
- Candlestick patterns: a study on Swedish stocks (2007–2015) using GARCH-M and bootstrapping found candlestick pattern strategies had **no predictive power**; separately, binomial tests on other candlestick studies likewise found patterns could not reliably predict market direction, and in forex markets there was "no net positive average return after transaction costs" even for complex multi-candle patterns — [The Predictive Power of Candlestick Patterns (Swedish market)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Predictive-Power-of-Candlestick-Patterns-An-of-J%C3%B6nsson/0a98a1374a5d563713ec49ba54b6f4adeaf474bc)
- Fibonacci retracements: academic/quantitative backtests comparing Fibonacci levels against randomly-placed control levels "find no statistically significant difference in hit rate," and researchers concluded "the significance of Fibonacci retracement levels could not be confirmed" — with perceived effectiveness attributed to psychological anchoring rather than genuine predictive content — [Fibonacci Retracement — Wikipedia summary of academic findings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_retracement); [Automatic identification and evaluation of Fibonacci retracements](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0957417421012495)

### Inferences
- The pattern across nearly every rigorous study is the same: in-sample / pre-transaction-cost results often look favorable, but out-of-sample performance and post-transaction-cost profitability collapse or disappear — this is the single most load-bearing finding for a dashboard-design decision.
- Signal "statistical significance" (as in Lo-Mamaysky-Wang) is a much weaker and more common finding than "net profitable trading edge after costs" — a dashboard should not conflate the two when deciding what to surface to a user.
- Evidence specifically for US equities (as opposed to FX/futures, where TA fares somewhat better per Park & Irwin) is the weakest link in the literature — most of the stronger positive within-asset-class results (FX, futures, emerging/developing equity markets) come from markets outside US large-cap equities.

### Gaps
- No post-2010 large-sample, pre-registered or multi-decade updated replication of Lo-Mamaysky-Wang specifically for recent US equities (e.g. 2000s–2020s) was found in this pass — the seminal evidence base is now 25+ years old and may not reflect how arbitraged-away these patterns are today given widespread algorithmic trading.
- The RSI/MACD findings surfaced directly contradict each other (one study: "all indicators perform poorly"; another: "several significant and profitable" with a 0.5% fee) and were not reconcilable within this research pass — flagged as an open contradiction rather than resolved.

## How much of retail "TA works" belief is driven by survivorship bias, cherry-picking, or overfitting? What do critics/skeptics say about its popularity despite weak evidence?

### Takeaway
Prominent academic critics (most notably Burton Malkiel in *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*) argue technical analysis is largely unreliable and persists due to psychological comfort and confirmation effects rather than genuine edge; quantitative finance literature independently documents that popularized/published trading rules reliably lose their edge after publication (a direct form of overfitting/crowding), and the behavioral-bias literature identifies survivorship bias and cherry-picking as structural mechanisms that make backtests and trader success stories look far more convincing than the underlying base rate actually is.

### Cited Findings
- Malkiel rejects technical analysis as "essentially meaningless," arguing it is inconsistent with weak-form market efficiency (all past price/volume data is already reflected in current prices) — [Random Walk Down Wall Street synopsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Random_Walk_Down_Wall_Street)
- Malkiel's famous demonstration: he generated a chart from pure coin-flip outcomes (no real trend) and showed it to a professional chartist, who nonetheless recommended buying the stock based on the "pattern" — illustrating that chartists can perceive meaningful structure in literal randomness — [Random Walk Down Wall Street synopsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Random_Walk_Down_Wall_Street)
- Malkiel directly tested several technical trading systems and reports finding no evidence that they were profitable, concluding most technical analysts fail to beat the market over the long run, "even though a few could strike it lucky in the near run" — [Random Walk Down Wall Street synopsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Random_Walk_Down_Wall_Street)
- The Bollinger Bands study found profitability was high before the indicator's publication and introduction (1983) and decayed after it became popular, especially after Bollinger's 2001 book — direct empirical evidence that publicizing an indicator erodes its edge, consistent with a "crowding"/arbitrage explanation of why popular TA tools underperform over time — [Popularity versus Profitability: Evidence from Bollinger Bands](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318717250_Popularity_versus_Profitability_Evidence_from_Bollinger_Bands)
- Sullivan, Timmermann & White's finding that the best in-sample rule (after data-snooping correction) failed out-of-sample is itself a textbook demonstration of overfitting: optimizing rule selection over a large rule universe on historical data produces results that look statistically significant but do not generalize — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=65140)
- General survivorship-bias literature notes that backtests run only on "surviving" stocks/strategies silently exclude failures (e.g., bankrupt companies or abandoned strategies), creating "the illusion of control" and leading to "confident, losing bets" — [Survivorship Bias: The Missing-Data Trap That Fools You](https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/survivorship-bias-abraham-wald-missing-data/)
- Confirmation bias and cherry-picking are described in the general bias literature as mechanisms whereby people selectively seek and overweight evidence supporting existing beliefs while discounting contradicting evidence — directly applicable to how retail traders interpret ambiguous chart signals as confirming a pattern they already expect — [Cherry-picking information (bioRxiv)](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.06.29.450332.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- The decay-after-publication pattern (Bollinger Bands) generalizes as a strong a priori reason to expect that any widely-taught, YouTube-popularized TA signal has already had much of its edge arbitraged away by the time a retail user adopts it — this is a stronger and more specific mechanism than generic "markets are efficient" hand-waving.
- Overfitting in retail content specifically likely manifests as: backtesting a pattern/indicator over a cherry-picked historical window or a small set of tickers, without out-of-sample validation or multiple-testing correction — exactly the failure mode that Sullivan-Timmermann-White's bootstrap was designed to detect and that academic studies without such correction are criticized for.

### Gaps
- No direct empirical study specifically quantifying survivorship bias *within retail TA YouTube/finance-content track records* (e.g., analysis of how often "successful trader" content survives vs. disappears) was found; the survivorship-bias evidence found is general/behavioral-finance literature, not TA-content-specific. This should be noted as an inference rather than a directly cited finding.

## Are there any specific technical signals that have held up reasonably well in rigorous, out-of-sample testing, versus ones that are essentially folklore?

### Takeaway
Of all the classic techniques reviewed, none has a strong, consistently-replicated, out-of-sample, post-transaction-cost edge specifically on US equities; the relatively "least folklore" results are modest statistical-significance findings for certain chart patterns (Lo-Mamaysky-Wang on Nasdaq) and some head-and-shoulders evidence in less-efficient small-cap (Russell 2000) names, while Fibonacci retracements, candlestick patterns, and Bollinger Bands look closest to pure folklore once tested against random controls or post-cost performance.

### Cited Findings
- Strongest (relatively) evidence: Lo-Mamaysky-Wang found statistically significant incremental information from chart patterns, especially for Nasdaq stocks (all 10 of 10 patterns significant) vs. weaker results for NYSE/AMEX (5 of 10) — but again, statistical significance ≠ profitable trading strategy — [NBER Working Paper 7613](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w7613/w7613.pdf)
- Head-and-shoulders: weak evidence of profitability specifically in Russell 2000 (smaller, less efficiently-priced) stocks, with the researchers suggesting it "may work well in designing hedged portfolios" even though it's "likely unprofitable as a standalone trading strategy in rising markets" — [Predictive Power of Head-and-Shoulders in the US Stock Market](https://www.biz.uiowa.edu/faculty/gsavin/papers/hsrevision_paw_10%2019%2006.pdf)
- Essentially folklore: Fibonacci retracements show no statistically significant difference in "hit rate" versus randomly-placed control levels in backtests — i.e., performance indistinguishable from chance — [Fibonacci Retracement — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_retracement)
- Essentially folklore (post-cost): Bollinger Bands are "consistently unable to earn profits in excess of the buy-and-hold" strategy once transaction costs are included — [Popularity versus Profitability: Evidence from Bollinger Bands](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318717250_Popularity_versus_Profitability_Evidence_from_Bollinger_Bands)
- Essentially folklore: candlestick patterns showed no predictive power in rigorous Swedish-market testing (GARCH-M + bootstrap) and no net positive return after transaction costs in forex testing, though a few individual patterns (e.g., Piercing pattern in Taiwan, Hammer/Bullish Engulfing in a real-estate-market study) showed isolated positive results in other, non-US-equity contexts — making the evidence inconsistent and context-dependent rather than a robust, generalizable edge — [Predictive Power of Candlestick Patterns (Swedish)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Predictive-Power-of-Candlestick-Patterns-An-of-J%C3%B6nsson/0a98a1374a5d563713ec49ba54b6f4adeaf474bc)
- Support/resistance: some out-of-sample-style evidence of a "memory effect" — price levels with more prior bounces show a stronger subsequent relationship to future bounces, and round-number levels (multiples of 100 on the Dow) act as psychological, at-least-temporary support/resistance — this is one of the few TA concepts with some structural (not just statistical-fluke) behavioral rationale, though profitability of trading on it varies across the international markets tested (JSE, Nasdaq, CAC 40, Nikkei 225, DAX, BIST100) — [Evidence and Behaviour of Support and Resistance Levels (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/html/2101.07410v1)
- Moving-average crossovers retain a real but modest and cost-fragile edge: MA strategies show lower raw average returns/Sharpe ratios than buy-and-hold but higher CAPM alpha in some studies (a risk-adjusted, not raw-return, advantage), while slippage/transaction costs eliminate profitability in the majority (>65%) of parameter combinations tested in other work — a genuinely mixed, parameter-sensitive result rather than clean folklore or clean validation — [Testing moving-average rules as a portfolio selection strategy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927538X12000327); [DMAC strategy analysis](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Teaching/BA453_2002/CCAM/CCAM.htm)

### Inferences
- If forced to rank by "least folklore-like" for US equities specifically: (1) moving-average-based signals have the most nuanced, risk-adjusted-but-fragile support; (2) chart-pattern statistical-significance (Lo-Mamaysky-Wang) and support/resistance "memory effects" have modest structural/behavioral rationale; (3) Fibonacci retracements and most candlestick patterns have the weakest evidence, performing no better than randomly-chosen control levels/patterns in rigorous tests.
- For a dashboard, this suggests that if any classic TA signal is included at all, it should be framed as a weak, context-dependent input (e.g., one modest feature among many, risk-adjusted rather than raw-return framed) rather than a standalone "buy/sell" signal — consistent with how the academic literature itself frames its strongest findings (CAPM alpha advantage, not raw outperformance; hedged-portfolio use, not standalone shorting).

### Gaps
- No rigorous, recent (post-2015), large-sample out-of-sample study isolating RSI or MACD specifically on broad US equity indices (as opposed to FX, single-index futures, or non-US markets) was found in this pass; the two RSI/MACD sources found directly conflict and neither is confirmed to be US-equities-specific or recent. This is a genuine gap rather than a resolved finding — flag as needing further research if RSI/MACD inclusion is being considered for the dashboard.
