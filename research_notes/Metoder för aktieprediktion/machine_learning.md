# Machine Learning / AI Approaches for US Stock Price Prediction

## What are the common ML approaches used for stock prediction, and how do they work in plain terms?

### Takeaway
The field splits into five families — classical ML on engineered features (random forest, gradient boosting/XGBoost), deep sequence models (LSTM/GRU), transformers, reinforcement learning (RL) for trading policies, and NLP/sentiment models — but the strongest, most reproducible academic evidence favors boosted trees and random forests on engineered cross-sectional features (price, volume, fundamentals), not raw-sequence deep learning; LSTM/transformer papers on stock prediction are disproportionately affected by leakage and routinely fail to beat a naive "no-change" baseline once validated correctly.

### Cited Findings
- Classical ML (random forests, boosted regression trees) and neural networks were the best-performing methods in a large systematic comparison of ML methods for asset pricing, gaining predictive advantage specifically from modeling nonlinear interactions between predictors (e.g., momentum × volatility) that linear models miss — [Gu, Kelly, Xiu "Empirical Asset Pricing via Machine Learning", NBER / Review of Financial Studies](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25398/w25398.pdf)
- All ML methods in that study agreed on the same dominant predictive signals: variations on momentum, liquidity, and volatility — i.e., the same families of signals already used in rule-based technical approaches — [Gu, Kelly, Xiu](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3159577)
- LSTM/attention models are described repeatedly across the literature as "very prone to overfitting" on stock data, with validation loss increasing while training accuracy keeps improving, and attention mechanisms sometimes having more parameters than there are data points — [search synthesis of multiple arXiv/MDPI papers on LSTM stock prediction overfitting](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/12/7/945)
- A direct empirical test found an LSTM trained on daily OHLCV data could not reliably beat a naive "tomorrow = today" baseline; every tested model variant landed within 0.02% MAPE of the naive persistence forecast — [GitHub stock-predictor LSTM baseline comparison project](https://github.com/luwymna/stock-predictor); broader academic review corroborates that deep models are "frequently bested by the most basic persistence benchmark" on raw price series.
- XGBoost/gradient boosting on engineered technical features is widely used and, under walk-forward validation, is reported to "often outperform other approaches on tabular financial data with hand-crafted features" due to tree models' inductive bias toward nonlinear feature interactions — [search synthesis, walk-forward GBM stock literature](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/15/6/1334) (Regime-Aware LightGBM paper)
- Reported XGBoost directional accuracy varies wildly across papers: one reports 73% on OMXS30 trend classification, another reports accuracy "close to random chance" at 50.24%, and a general rule of thumb cited is that any reported ~70%+ accuracy on financial direction prediction "should raise suspicion" of look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, or data leakage — [search synthesis of XGBoost stock prediction papers](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1531990/FULLTEXT02)
- Random forest papers report suspiciously high accuracy (84.89% to "over 99%" across multiple stocks) for next-period direction prediction — these figures are flagged in this research as a red flag for leakage/overfitting rather than evidence of a working edge, since accuracy this high on a near-random-walk target is not plausible without some form of information leakage — [search synthesis, random forest stock prediction papers](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356765234_Research_on_stock_trend_prediction_method_based_on_optimized_random_forest)
- Transformer-based models for stock prediction face documented weaknesses: degraded performance on volatile/chaotic price segments, inability to handle high-frequency non-systematic fluctuations despite global attention, high computational cost, and in head-to-head comparisons "only a slight increase in model performance" over classical ML despite much higher training cost — making them hard to justify "as compared to traditional machine learning models" — [search synthesis of transformer stock prediction limitation papers](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0316955)
- Reinforcement learning for trading (e.g., FinRL-style frameworks) trains an agent (often PPO, DDPG, TD3) to learn a trading policy directly rather than a price forecast; a known failure mode is that most open-source RL trading environments assume negligible/fixed transaction costs, which lets agents learn behaviors that look profitable in backtest but fail under realistic costs — in one study, PPO's out-of-sample return dropped from the baseline-reported level to 25% once a realistic nonlinear market-impact model (Almgren–Chriss framework) was applied, while TD3 improved from 24% to 30%, showing cost modeling can flip which algorithm looks best — [Realistic Market Impact Modeling for RL Trading Environments, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.29086)
- RL-for-trading papers are explicitly flagged for the same backtest-overfitting problem as other ML trading work: "existing works applied deep RL methods and optimistically reported increased profits in backtesting, which may suffer from the false positive issue due to overfitting" — [arXiv 2209.05559, Deep RL for Cryptocurrency Trading: Practical Approach to Address Backtest Overfitting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05559)
- Sentiment/NLP approaches (FinBERT, lexicon-based scoring on news/Twitter) are claimed by many papers to correlate with price moves — a widely-cited (and now dated/contested) example is Bollen, Mao & Zeng's claim that Twitter mood predicted Dow Jones moves with "87.6% accuracy" — [search synthesis, Twitter sentiment stock prediction literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11157597/)

### Inferences
- The pattern across methods is consistent: feature-engineered classical ML (trees/boosting) on structured price/volume/fundamental data has the most credible, reproducible evidence of adding value (Gu-Kelly-Xiu), while raw-sequence deep learning (LSTM, transformers) attracts far more hype-driven papers with suspiciously high accuracy that likely reflects leakage, and is intrinsically harder to validate correctly because sequence models are more sensitive to how normalization/splitting is done.
- The claim that Twitter sentiment predicts DJIA with 87.6% accuracy (Bollen et al., 2010/2011) is an old, heavily-cited result that should be treated with real caution: it has not been the basis of any durable trading edge in the 15 years since, consistent with the broader finding that correlational sentiment-price studies rarely survive out-of-sample/cost-aware testing. Treat it as a famous but unreplicated headline stat, not a settled fact.
- RL for trading is the least mature and most backtest-overfitting-prone category of the five, largely because the agent is optimizing directly against a backtest environment (reward hacking against unrealistic cost/liquidity assumptions) rather than making a falsifiable, interpretable price forecast.

### Gaps
- Could not verify current replication status / independent critique of the original Bollen-Mao-Zeng Twitter-DJIA study within the tool budget for this task; this is a well-known, frequently-cited figure but its real predictive value out-of-sample is contested in later literature that this research pass did not fully trace down.
- Did not find a direct head-to-head paper comparing gradient boosting vs. a true rule-based trend/momentum/volume formula (the kind already used in the dashboard this report is informing) under identical, leakage-free, cost-aware conditions. The closest proxy evidence is Gu-Kelly-Xiu showing all ML methods converge on momentum/liquidity/volatility as the dominant signals — suggesting ML's edge over a well-built rule-based momentum system, if any, is more about better combining/weighting those same signals than discovering wholly new information.

## What data does each approach realistically need — how much is achievable with free data (Yahoo Finance) vs. expensive data (tick/order-book/alt-data/large text corpora)?

### Takeaway
Classical ML (random forest, XGBoost) on daily OHLCV + simple fundamentals (SEC EDGAR) and engineered technical features is the one approach fully achievable with free data; LSTM/transformer sequence models are nominally achievable on free daily data too but need far more historical history and careful regularization to avoid overfitting a small dataset; sentiment/NLP needs large labeled text corpora and/or paid news-sentiment feeds to be done well; RL-for-trading and anything claiming an edge from microstructure needs tick-level or order-book data that is not free.

### Cited Findings
- Gu-Kelly-Xiu's large-scale ML asset-pricing study is built on broad, inexpensive, widely available inputs: monthly/daily price-based characteristics (momentum, volatility, liquidity) plus firm fundamentals — not tick data or alternative data — and still finds large economic gains vs. simple regression benchmarks — [Gu, Kelly, Xiu](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25398/w25398.pdf)
- LSTM and attention-based stock models are repeatedly noted in the literature as needing "much more data" than is typically available, with researchers stating "it is very easy to overfit with LSTM and convolutional network models, implying much more data is needed" — a single ticker's free daily history (years, not tick-level) is often insufficient for these architectures' parameter counts — [search synthesis, LSTM overfitting literature](https://cs230.stanford.edu/projects_winter_2020/reports/32066186.pdf)
- RL trading research increasingly focuses on realistic market-impact and order-book-level modeling (Almgren-Chriss square-root impact law) to avoid reward-hacking artifacts of frictionless backtests — this class of realism requires order-book/liquidity data that goes beyond free daily OHLCV — [Realistic Market Impact Modeling for RL Trading Environments](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.29086v1)
- Sentiment/NLP pipelines in the academic literature combine FinBERT/LSTM with "financial news articles and Twitter posts collected over 24-month periods" — i.e., they require either a paid news/social API, scraped corpora, or a research-only dataset; free, broad, long-history financial news corpora are not generally available the way free daily price history is — [search synthesis, sentiment-LSTM stock prediction papers](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440251415069)

### Inferences
- For a dashboard currently limited to Finnhub live quotes, Yahoo Finance daily history, and SEC EDGAR filings, the only ML family with a realistic free-data path and credible supporting evidence is classical ML (random forest / gradient boosting) on engineered technical + fundamental features. LSTM is technically possible on the same free data but is the worst-fit architecture for this data volume (daily bars on a modest number of tickers), given its documented hunger for more data than that provides. Transformers are even more data-hungry and compute-heavy and are not a good match. RL-for-trading and NLP/sentiment both require data categories (order-book/impact modeling data; large text corpora) that are outside the "Finnhub + Yahoo + EDGAR" free stack.

### Gaps
- No direct source was found quantifying a specific minimum data volume/number of tickers needed before classical ML (RF/XGBoost) reliably beats a rule-based baseline on daily data; this would need to be established empirically rather than from a paper.
- Could not find a clear free-data-only sentiment proxy (e.g., free EDGAR 8-K/10-K text, free limited news) that has documented predictive evidence comparable to the paid-news/Twitter studies above; this is a specific gap for anyone trying to do sentiment ML using only EDGAR text instead of paid news/social feeds.

## What is the single biggest, most commonly cited methodological failure mode in ML stock-prediction research?

### Takeaway
There is no single universal "biggest" failure mode — it's a cluster of three recurring ones that compound each other: (1) backtest overfitting from testing many models/hyperparameters on the same data (formalized by Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu's Probability of Backtest Overfitting), (2) data leakage via normalization/scaling computed over the full dataset before train/test split or via temporal leakage in cross-validation, and (3) survivorship bias in the stock universe tested. López de Prado explicitly lists survivorship bias first among recurring backtest flaws, and separately argues most financial-ML failures trace to bad data handling and bad testing rather than bad models.

### Cited Findings
- Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu, "The Probability of Backtest Overfitting" (Journal of Computational Finance, 2015/SSRN) formalize backtest overfitting as the probability that a strategy chosen as the in-sample "best" among N tested configurations performs below the out-of-sample median; the expected out-of-sample rank of the in-sample winner declines sharply as the number of configurations tested (N) grows — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2326253); their worked numeric example (Figure 2) shows configurations with in-sample Sharpe ratios of 1–3 had about 78% negative out-of-sample Sharpe ratios, and the estimated Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO) for that example reached 74% — [summary of Bailey et al., aifinhub.io](https://aifinhub.io/learn/bailey-lopez-pbo/)
- The authors propose combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV) to estimate PBO without needing a separate holdout set, and recommend rejecting strategies with estimated PBO materially above a low threshold (the summarizing source cites "0.05" as a rejection bar and notes PBO > 0.5 means the in-sample "winner" did worse than a randomly chosen configuration) — [aifinhub.io summary of Bailey et al.](https://aifinhub.io/learn/bailey-lopez-pbo/) — note this is a secondary summary, not the primary SSRN text itself, which this research pass was not able to fetch directly.
- López de Prado's "The 10 Reasons Most Machine Learning Funds Fail" lists survivorship bias first among recurring backtest flaws, and separately diagnoses backtest overfitting from "a not strictly planned loop of sequential testing and 'improving' a strategy" as leading to "a significant overfitting bias that will likely render the result worthless" — [López de Prado, SSRN "10 Reasons"](https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/SSRN-id3104816.pdf); his broader diagnosis is that financial data is "noisy ... non-stationary ... and adversarial (once a pattern is found, it tends to disappear)," and that cross-validation without "purging" of overlapping time windows lets a model indirectly "see the answer" — [search synthesis of Lopez de Prado's Advances in Financial Machine Learning](https://reasonabledeviations.com/notes/adv_fin_ml/)
- Survivorship bias is defined and flagged as critical specifically because "the universe you test on has been curated by the very outcome you are trying to measure" — a backtest universe of only currently-listed stocks systematically excludes historical losers/delistings, overstating historical returns before any model is even applied — [search synthesis, survivorship bias in backtesting](https://algoseek.com/blog/data-guides/survivorship-bias-in-backtesting-what-to-ask-a-data-vendor/)
- Normalization leakage is independently documented as a widespread, specific leakage mechanism: "When you standardize an entire dataset before splitting it, the scaler learns its mean and standard deviation from the entire dataset, including observations from the future test period" — the fix is computing scaling statistics using only the training/past window — [search synthesis on normalization leakage](https://www.newsletter.quantreo.com/p/look-ahead-bias-the-invisible-killer)
- A 2026 systematic literature review of AI/ML stock-market-prediction papers (2016–2026) finds the field has a near-absence of statistical hypothesis testing and documents multiple recurring, mathematically-formalized leakage mechanisms (including what it calls a "Lag-1 Shadowing Trap") that call into question whether reported performance gains are credible — [Systematic Literature Review of AI/ML for Stock Market Prediction, Research Square 2026](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-11084191/v1) — note: this research pass could only access the title/abstract-level summary via search snippets, not the full methodology text, so the exact mechanics of the "Lag-1 Shadowing Trap" could not be independently verified here.

### Inferences
- These three failure modes compound: a researcher who tests many feature sets/architectures (backtest overfitting), on a convenience universe of surviving large-caps (survivorship bias), using a scaler fit on the whole dataset (normalization leakage) can produce a backtest that looks like a genuine edge but is actually three layers of statistical illusion stacked together. This is consistent with why reported stock-direction accuracies above ~65-70% should be treated as a red flag rather than a result to be excited about, per the XGBoost accuracy discussion above.
- For a solo developer, the practically most dangerous of the three is normalization/scaling leakage, because it is the easiest to introduce by accident (e.g., calling a single `scaler.fit_transform()` on the full time series before any split) and the hardest to notice, since the resulting backtest numbers look perfectly plausible rather than absurd.

### Gaps
- Could not directly access the full primary text of Bailey et al.'s SSRN paper (site returned non-fetchable content); the PBO numeric example (78% negative OOS Sharpe, 74% PBO) is sourced from a third-party summary (aifinhub.io) rather than verified against the original paper's Figure 2 directly. This should be treated as high-confidence but secondary-sourced.
- Could not verify the specific technical definition of the "Lag-1 Shadowing Trap" cited in the 2026 systematic review beyond its mention in search snippets; the full paper was not accessible for this research pass.

## Is there credible evidence that any ML approach meaningfully beats simple baselines net of realistic trading costs, in true out-of-sample tests?

### Takeaway
The evidence is split by claim type: (a) at the research level, ML forecasts of expected returns/risk premia do show genuine, replicated economic value over linear-regression baselines (Gu-Kelly-Xiu), and some ML trading strategies remain profitable net of realistic transaction costs when the trading rule is cost-aware; but (b) the specific, narrower claim that "ML predicts next-day price direction well enough to trade profitably" is largely not supported — most such papers report backtest accuracy that collapses once leakage is fixed, costs are applied, or validation is done honestly, and naive high-turnover ML signals "collapse after transaction costs because they trade too frequently."

### Cited Findings
- Gu-Kelly-Xiu report ML return forecasts deliver "large economic gains to investors... in some cases doubling the performance of leading regression-based strategies from the literature," with trees and neural nets gaining their edge from nonlinear interactions among momentum/liquidity/volatility signals — this is evidence at the level of return/risk-premia forecasting, a different (longer-horizon, portfolio-level) task than daily direction prediction — [Gu, Kelly, Xiu, NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25398/w25398.pdf)
- A cost-aware ML strategy literature finds "more sophisticated machine learning strategies are profitable, earning net out-of-sample monthly returns of up to 1.42%, despite having turnover rates exceeding 50%," and that "most machine learning combination strategies continue to deliver significant returns net of costs despite one-sided turnover ranging between 60% and 70%" — [search synthesis citing AFA "Expected Returns on Machine-Learning Strategies" and related work](https://afajof.org/management/viewp.php?n=75544)
- The same literature finds the opposite for naive signals: "naive sign-based strategies generate positive gross performance in selected configurations, yet they collapse after transaction costs because they trade too frequently," and more broadly "after incorporating realistic transaction costs, only a limited subset of assets remained profitable" — the determining factor is whether the trading rule itself is cost-aware (e.g., only trading when predicted edge exceeds a cost threshold), not the raw predictive accuracy of the model — [search synthesis, ML trading cost literature](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/applied-mathematics-and-statistics/articles/10.3389/fams.2026.1888767/full)
- On the narrower "predict next-day direction and trade it" claim: daily prices are close to a random walk, and even an LSTM "cannot reliably beat the naive 'no change' baseline" once evaluated honestly — meaning the raw predictive signal many retail ML tutorials chase (next-bar direction from price history alone) shows little to no evidence of being real — [search synthesis, LSTM vs random-walk baseline literature](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9427226/)
- RL trading backtests are explicitly called out as prone to "false positive" profit claims from overfitting to the backtest, and realistic transaction-cost/impact modeling can materially change which RL algorithm appears best or even flip a strategy from profitable to unprofitable — [arXiv 2209.05559](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05559); [arXiv 2603.29086](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.29086v1)
- XGBoost/gradient boosting direction-accuracy results range from near-random (50.24%) to suspiciously high (73%, even claims near 99% in some random forest papers), with the research-synthesis explicitly flagging that "a 70% accuracy result in financial data should raise suspicion about potential issues like look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, data leakage" — reinforcing that headline accuracy numbers in this literature are not a reliable signal of real tradeable edge — [search synthesis, XGBoost accuracy papers](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1531990/FULLTEXT02)

### Inferences
- The credible evidence for ML "working" in finance is almost entirely at the level of (a) longer-horizon expected-return/factor forecasting using broad cross-sections of stocks and many engineered features (an institutional, portfolio-construction problem), and (b) cost-aware strategy design, where the trading rule itself accounts for transaction costs rather than acting on every small predicted edge. It is not strong at the level of "take one ticker's daily OHLCV, predict tomorrow's direction, and trade on it" — which is the use case most relevant to a simple rule-based trend/momentum/volume dashboard. This suggests that if ML is added, its honest framing should be "a better way to combine/weight the same momentum/volume/trend signals already in use across a broader universe of stocks," not "a new source of predictive information from price history alone."
- The gap between "77% test accuracy in a paper" and "profitable after costs" is best explained by: overfitting to the backtest (PBO), leakage inflating the accuracy number itself, and the fact that a classifier can have above-50% directional accuracy and still lose money once trading costs, slippage, and position sizing are applied — none of the sources found directly quantify this gap in dollar terms for a retail-scale account, which is a gap below.

### Gaps
- Did not find a source directly comparing a real rule-based momentum/trend/volume signal (the kind this dashboard already runs) against an ML model on identical data/costs/period — the comparison has to be inferred indirectly from Gu-Kelly-Xiu's finding that all methods converge on momentum/liquidity/volatility signals.
- Did not find retail-scale (small account, free data, single-ticker-at-a-time) net-of-cost profitability studies — the profitable cost-aware ML strategies cited above are from academic/institutional multi-asset portfolio contexts with turnover and cost assumptions that may not map directly onto a personal dashboard trading a handful of US equities.

## What would be a realistic, honestly-scoped ML experiment for a solo developer with free data and no large compute budget?

### Takeaway
Realistic: gradient boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM) or random forest classifying short-term direction (e.g., next 1-5 day return sign, or a 3-class up/flat/down) from engineered technical features (returns, moving averages, RSI, volume changes, volatility) computed from free Yahoo Finance daily data, evaluated with walk-forward (expanding-window, purged) validation and compared against the existing rule-based signal and a buy-and-hold baseline, with transaction costs applied to any derived trading rule. Unrealistic for a solo developer on free data: training a transformer or large deep model from scratch, RL agents requiring realistic order-book/impact simulation, or any approach requiring tick-level, order-book, or large paid alternative-data/news corpora.

### Cited Findings
- Walk-forward validation ("walk forward optimization") is described as "now widely considered the 'gold standard' in trading strategy validation" and is explicitly recommended over a single train/test split for exactly this kind of individual-developer strategy testing — [Wikipedia: Walk forward optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walk_forward_optimization)
- Concrete walk-forward + GBM designs seen in the recent literature that are replicable with free daily data include: refitting a gradient boosting model "every 100 bars on the latest 300 samples, early-stopped on a later-in-time validation slice," and using "100 expanding-window folds with 10-day purge windows to prevent data leakage" — both are templates a solo developer could adapt without needing institutional infrastructure — [search synthesis, walk-forward GBM stock literature](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.13638)
- XGBoost incorporating "short- and medium-term rolling volatility measures and the 14-period Relative Strength Index" under "strict walk-forward validation" is a documented, free-data-compatible feature design (RSI and rolling volatility are both computable from Yahoo Finance OHLCV alone) — [search synthesis, XGBoost technical-indicator walk-forward literature](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/15/6/1334)
- Gradient boosting / tree ensembles are specifically noted to "often outperform other approaches on tabular financial data with hand-crafted features" — i.e., the realistic solo-developer approach (engineered features + boosted trees) is also the approach with the best methodological track record, not just the cheapest one — [search synthesis, same source as above]
- By contrast, transformer-based stock models are noted to have "extremely high" training time/computational cost relative to their marginal accuracy gain over classical ML, to the point that one comparison concluded "it was not beneficial to implement these models as compared to traditional machine learning models" — directly supporting that transformers are a poor fit for a no-large-compute-budget solo project — [search synthesis, transformer limitations literature](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0316955)
- RL-for-trading realism increasingly depends on market-impact/order-book modeling (Almgren-Chriss, square-root impact law) that requires data and simulation infrastructure beyond free daily OHLCV, and even well-resourced academic RL trading work is flagged for backtest-overfitting / false-positive profit claims — making RL a poor realistic choice for a solo developer on free data — [arXiv 2603.29086](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.29086v1); [arXiv 2209.05559](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05559)

### Inferences
- The single most defensible "add ML" experiment for this dashboard, given its existing free-data stack (Finnhub, Yahoo Finance daily, SEC EDGAR) and rule-based baseline, is: engineer the same categories of features the rule-based system already uses (trend, momentum, volume) plus a few standard technical indicators, feed them into a gradient boosting classifier predicting short-horizon direction, validate with purged walk-forward (not k-fold, not a single random split), and benchmark honestly against (a) buy-and-hold and (b) the existing rule-based formula, on the same tickers and period, before and after a realistic transaction-cost assumption. This mirrors the actual methodology used in the more credible papers found in this research (walk-forward GBM on engineered features) while avoiding the specific traps (full-dataset normalization, survivorship-biased universe, excessive architecture/hyperparameter search) repeatedly flagged as the field's core failure modes.
- Adding sentiment/NLP from SEC EDGAR text (free) is a plausible secondary free-data experiment, but there is no strong cited evidence in this research that EDGAR filing text specifically (as opposed to paid news/social feeds) has demonstrated predictive value for short-term price direction — this would be a genuinely exploratory extension, not a well-evidenced one.

### Gaps
- Did not find a worked, fully free-data (Yahoo Finance + SEC EDGAR only, no paid news/sentiment) reference implementation or published study matching this exact scoped experiment; the recommendation above is synthesized from adjacent literature (walk-forward GBM on technical indicators) rather than a single directly-on-point source.
- Did not find quantified minimum sample-size guidance (how many years of daily bars / how many tickers) needed for a gradient boosting model to produce a statistically meaningful signal rather than noise — this is a practical parameter a solo developer would need to determine empirically (e.g., via the PBO/CSCV framework from Bailey et al.) rather than from a single citable number.
