# Edge is rare, insider signals survive scrutiny

Across six decades of attempts to predict US stock movements — chart patterns, academic factors, machine learning, alternative data, fundamental screens, and algorithmic bots — credible evidence for a durable, cost-adjusted edge is the exception, not the rule, and the exceptions cluster narrowly around signals that are hard to arbitrage away: **opportunistic insider buying**, cost-aware combinations of already-known momentum/volatility signals, and a handful of price-only anomalies (MAX effect, idiosyncratic volatility) that have not yet decayed to zero. Classic technical analysis — moving averages, RSI, Fibonacci, candlesticks — consistently fails out-of-sample and post-transaction-cost tests, even when individual patterns show statistical significance ([NBER Working Paper 7613](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w7613/w7613.pdf)). Academic factor investing is real but decaying — McLean & Pontiff found published return predictors lose **58% of their returns post-publication** ([McLean & Pontiff](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12365)) — and the "factor zoo" literature is split over how much of it was ever real. Machine learning's credible wins are in feature engineering and cost-aware portfolio construction, not in raw-sequence models predicting next-day direction, where LSTMs cannot reliably beat a naive persistence baseline ([GitHub LSTM baseline study](https://github.com/luwymna/stock-predictor)). Alternative data is bifurcated: insider trading has genuine *Journal of Finance*-level support, while sentiment, options flow, and dark-pool signals are either methodologically shaky, reverse-causal, or locked behind paid feeds. And the retail finance-content ecosystem shows an inverted relationship between popularity and skill — the least accurate finfluencers attract the most followers ([Kakhbod et al., SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4428232)). For Candor, which has already rigorously tested five price/volume factors and found only insider-cluster-buying statistically significant, the evidence points toward refining that one working signal rather than searching for new ones, supplemented by two or three additional free-data factors with genuine academic grounding and a formal overfitting check on everything already built.

## Technical analysis collapses once transaction costs enter the picture

The retail technical-analysis toolkit — moving-average crossovers, MACD, RSI, Bollinger Bands, candlestick patterns, Fibonacci retracements, chart patterns like head-and-shoulders — is entirely computable from free daily OHLCV data (Yahoo Finance's `yfinance` library or Finnhub's free tier), meaning no paid feed is ever required to build any of it ([Yahoo Finance API Guide](https://scrapfly.io/blog/posts/guide-to-yahoo-finance-api)). The data access question is a non-issue; the predictive-power question is where the approach falls apart. Lo, Mamaysky & Wang's seminal 2000 study found several chart patterns statistically significant on Nasdaq stocks, but explicitly cautioned that statistical significance is not the same as a profitable trading strategy after costs ([NBER Working Paper 7613](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w7613/w7613.pdf)). Sullivan, Timmermann & White's bootstrap "Reality Check," built to correct for data-snooping across ~7,846 tested rules, found the best in-sample rule failed to outperform in the subsequent out-of-sample decade and showed no edge on S&P 500 futures once snooping bias was corrected ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=65140)). Park & Irwin's survey of 95 modern studies found only 56 reported positive profitability, and nearly all suffered from data-snooping or poor transaction-cost estimation — and critically, earlier work found technical analysis profitable in FX and futures but **not in stock markets specifically** ([Journal of Economic Surveys](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-6419.2007.00519.x)). The pattern repeats indicator by indicator: Bollinger Bands were profitable before their 1983 introduction and decayed sharply after popularization, becoming "consistently unable to earn profits in excess of buy-and-hold" after transaction costs ([Popularity versus Profitability](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/318717250_Popularity_versus_Profitability_Evidence_from_Bollinger_Bands)); Fibonacci retracements show no statistically significant difference in hit rate versus randomly placed control levels ([Fibonacci Retracement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_retracement)); candlestick patterns showed no predictive power under GARCH-M and bootstrap testing on Swedish equities ([Predictive Power of Candlestick Patterns](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Predictive-Power-of-Candlestick-Patterns-An-of-J%C3%B6nsson/0a98a1374a5d563713ec49ba54b6f4adeaf474bc)). Burton Malkiel's famous demonstration — showing a professional chartist a coin-flip-generated price chart, who recommended buying based on the "pattern" — captures why these tools persist despite the evidence: they feel diagnostic even when applied to pure noise ([A Random Walk Down Wall Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Random_Walk_Down_Wall_Street)). The closest thing to a surviving signal is moving-average crossovers, which show a modest, risk-adjusted (CAPM alpha) advantage in some studies even as raw profitability evaporates once slippage is applied to **over 65%** of parameter combinations tested ([DMAC analysis](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Teaching/BA453_2002/CCAM/CCAM.htm)) — a fragile, parameter-sensitive result, not a clean edge.

## Academic factor investing works in principle, decays in practice

Beyond the five factors Candor has already tested, the canonical academic factor set includes value (HML), quality (QMJ), investment (CMA), accruals, payout yield, post-earnings-announcement drift, short interest, net share issuance, and the MAX/idiosyncratic-volatility effect. The central complication for a free-data dashboard is that most of the fundamentals-based factors — value, quality, investment, accruals, payout — require **point-in-time historical fundamentals** (as they would have been known on the filing date, before restatements) that free vendor snapshots like Finnhub do not provide; SEC EDGAR's XBRL companyfacts API technically contains this data for free, but assembling a clean, restatement-safe panel from heterogeneous tagging across thousands of filers is a substantial engineering project, not a quick integration. A smaller set of factors sidesteps this entirely because they are either pure price/volume signals or derived from SEC filings that are naturally point-in-time by construction: the MAX effect (Bali, Cakici & Whitelaw found stocks with the highest max daily return in the trailing month underperform the lowest-MAX stocks by up to **1.03% per month**, computable from Yahoo price history alone) ([MAX effect paper](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~rwhitela/papers/max%20jfe11.pdf)), net share issuance/buyback (companies issuing shares subsequently underperform repurchasers, with an international quintile spread of **0.85%/month**, extractable from XBRL shares-outstanding tags) ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X09001007)), and Form 4 insider transactions, filed within days of the actual trade. The replication-crisis literature is itself divided: Hou, Xue & Zhang replicated ~450 published anomalies and found roughly **65% fail** to clear a t-stat of 1.96 once microcap stocks are properly weighted, since microcaps are only ~3% of total market cap but ~60% of listed names and disproportionately drive spurious results ([Replicating Anomalies](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/345507035_Replicating_Anomalies)); yet Chen & Zimmermann and Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen report 98–100% reproduction rates once methodology is held constant, attributing Hou-Xue-Zhang's lower figure to weighting and breakpoint choices rather than the anomalies being fake ([factor zoo primer](https://skybluerw.github.io/2024/12/07/factor-zoo.html)). McLean & Pontiff's study of 97 return predictors found returns were **26% lower out-of-sample** than in-sample and **58% lower post-publication**, with roughly 32 percentage points of that decline attributable specifically to publication-driven arbitrage ([McLean & Pontiff](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12365)). Most sobering for Candor's own results: Linnainmaa & Roberts, using hand-collected accounting data back to the early 20th century, found that profitability and investment — exactly the RMW/CMA factors added in the Fama-French five-factor model — show **no statistically reliable premium** in the pre-1963 period outside their original discovery sample ([Linnainmaa & Roberts](https://rodneywhitecenter.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/12-16.Roberts.pdf)). Given that Candor already found no edge in five well-documented factors, this is a reasonable prior to carry forward: a meaningful share of the remaining factor zoo may be sample-specific rather than persistent, and any newly-found "significant" factor should be checked for concentration in illiquid/microcap names before being trusted, since a free-data dashboard with no liquidity filtering is structurally prone to exactly the bias Hou-Xue-Zhang identify.

## Machine learning rewards feature engineering, not sequence models

Gu, Kelly & Xiu's large-scale comparison of ML methods for asset pricing found random forests and boosted trees were the best performers, deriving their edge from modeling nonlinear interactions between predictors — momentum crossed with volatility, for instance — that linear models miss, and importantly, every ML method in their study converged on the **same dominant signals**: variations on momentum, liquidity, and volatility, the same families already used in rule-based approaches ([Gu, Kelly, Xiu](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25398/w25398.pdf)). This is the single most important finding for Candor: ML's credible advantage is in better *combining and weighting* known signals, not discovering new information from price history alone. The contrast with raw-sequence deep learning is stark. LSTM and attention-based models are repeatedly documented as prone to severe overfitting on stock data, with validation loss rising even as training accuracy improves; a direct empirical test found an LSTM trained on daily OHLCV could not reliably beat a naive "tomorrow equals today" baseline, landing within 0.02% MAPE of pure persistence ([GitHub LSTM study](https://github.com/luwymna/stock-predictor)). Reported directional-accuracy figures across the ML stock-prediction literature should themselves raise suspicion: results range from near-random (50.24%) to implausibly high (73%, with some random forest papers claiming over 99%), and the field's own rule of thumb is that any reported accuracy above roughly 70% on financial direction prediction signals look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, or data leakage rather than a real edge ([diva-portal XGBoost study](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1531990/FULLTEXT02)). Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu formalized this as the "Probability of Backtest Overfitting" (PBO): in their worked example, strategies with in-sample Sharpe ratios of 1–3 had roughly **78% negative out-of-sample Sharpe ratios**, with an estimated PBO of 74% ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2326253)). The gap between headline accuracy and real profitability is explained by three compounding failure modes López de Prado identifies as recurring: survivorship-biased universes, normalization/scaling leakage (fitting a scaler on the full dataset before splitting, which lets the model see future statistics), and uncontrolled backtest overfitting from testing many configurations ([López de Prado, "10 Reasons"](https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/SSRN-id3104816.pdf)). Where ML does show a genuine, replicated net-of-cost edge is in cost-aware strategy design at the institutional/portfolio level — strategies earning net monthly returns up to 1.42% despite turnover above 50%, because the trading rule itself only acts when predicted edge exceeds a cost threshold, rather than trading on every small signal ([AFA cost-aware ML strategies](https://afajof.org/management/viewp.php?n=75544)). For a solo developer on free data, the realistic, defensible experiment is gradient boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM) on engineered technical features — returns, moving averages, RSI, volume, volatility — validated with purged, expanding-window walk-forward testing, benchmarked honestly against both buy-and-hold and the existing rule-based signal, with transaction costs applied throughout.

## Insider trading is the one alternative-data signal with real teeth

Among sentiment, options flow, dark-pool data, and insider trading, only insider trading has robust, primary, top-journal academic support that directly maps onto free data Candor already has. Lakonishok & Lee's benchmark study (NYSE/AMEX/Nasdaq, 1975–1995) found insider **buying** carries real predictive information while insider **selling** does not, with heavy-buying firms outperforming heavy-selling firms by **7.8%** over the following 12 months (4.8% after controlling for size and book-to-market), concentrated in smaller firms ([Lakonishok & Lee](https://www.lsvasset.com/pdf/research-papers/Insider-Trades-Informative.pdf)). The more important refinement comes from Cohen, Malloy & Pomorski's *Journal of Finance* paper "Decoding Inside Information": more than half of all insider trades are "routine" — scheduled, recurring, often 10b5-1 plan executions — and carry **zero** predictive power, while a portfolio trading only on identified "opportunistic" (non-routine) trades earns abnormal returns of **82 basis points per month**, versus essentially zero for routine trades ([NBER version](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16454/w16454.pdf)). Critically, the most informative opportunistic insiders are not senior executives at large firms but **local, non-executive insiders at geographically concentrated, poorly-governed firms** — meaning a factor that overweights by seniority (e.g., CEO buys) is weighting the wrong dimension; routineness and firm opacity matter more than title. This is directly actionable: Candor's existing insider-cluster factor is almost certainly diluted by routine trades mixed into the sample, and filtering them out using the same free EDGAR data is a concrete, well-evidenced upgrade rather than a new, untested idea. By contrast, the other three alternative-data categories are weaker fits. Sentiment shows strong evidence of **reverse causality** — volatility drives discussion volume more than the reverse in several studies ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1057521924005751)) — and effects that do exist are concentrated in meme-stock episodes and fade within days; meanwhile the free data infrastructure has degraded, with Pushshift (the source most academic Reddit-sentiment research relied on) losing broad API access in 2023 and StockTwits' API frozen to new developers ([socialcrawl.dev](https://www.socialcrawl.dev/blog/reddit-data-api-2026)). Unusual options activity has genuine academic support — strongest around pre-takeover call buying, where roughly 25% of takeover targets show abnormal options volume beforehand — but publicized unusual activity (the CNBC-style, retail-alert-service version) shows overreaction followed by **reversal**, not continuation, and real-time options data carries mandatory OPRA licensing fees even for non-professional retail use (~$1.25–$31.50/user/month, or $4,500/month for direct feed access) ([marketdata.app](https://www.marketdata.app/education/options/opra-fees/)). Dark-pool data is free only in a weekly, roughly two-week-delayed aggregate form from FINRA — useful for slow structural research, useless for a timing signal — while real-time, ticker-level dark-pool prints are bundled into the same paid products (starting at $50/month) as options flow ([Unusual Whales pricing](https://unusualwhales.com/pricing)).

## Fundamental screens and retail bots fail the same honesty test

Common retail fundamental playbooks — low P/E or P/B screens, Greenblatt's Magic Formula, Lynch-style GARP/PEG, dividend-growth screens — share the same structural problem as the blocked academic value/quality factors: an honest backtest requires restatement-corrected, point-in-time historical fundamentals, which exist only as paid products (Compustat, Sharadar, Eulerpool); free snapshot data applied to historical dates is implicitly look-ahead-biased. Greenblatt's original Magic Formula backtest showed ~30.8% annualized returns, but no independent study has replicated anything close to that, and over the five years ending 2024 the strategy **underperformed the S&P 500 by roughly 6 points annualized** ([quant-investing.com](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/magic-formula-investment-strategy-back-test)). This matters beyond fundamentals screening specifically: it means content creators publishing multi-decade value/GARP backtests are, by construction, either using undisclosed paid data, applying today's snapshot data to past dates, or working from survivor-only universes — there is no honest free way to do it, which is itself a reason for structural skepticism toward retail fundamentals-backtest content. The algorithmic-bot side of the ecosystem tells a parallel story. A genuinely free-to-low-cost stack exists — Python libraries (backtesting.py, vectorbt, Zipline-reloaded) paired with commission-free brokers (Alpaca, Interactive Brokers) or an all-in-one platform (QuantConnect) — but across every independent source consulted, the dominant theme is that strategies which backtest well routinely fail live due to overfitting, slippage, latency, and regime change, with TradingView's Pine Script specifically flagged for structural issues like repainting and lookahead bias from premature higher-timeframe data access ([blog.traderspost.io](https://blog.traderspost.io/article/pine-script-backtesting-limitations-and-validation)). The clearest, best-evidenced finding in this entire research arc concerns the finance-content ecosystem itself: a peer-reviewed-track study using StockTwits data on over 29,000 financial influencers found roughly 56% are "antiskilled" (generating **-2.3% monthly abnormal returns**) while only 28% are genuinely skilled (+2.6% monthly) — and critically, **antiskilled influencers have more followers and more influence on retail trading than skilled ones** ([Kakhbod et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4428232)). Barber & Odean's study of 66,465 US discount-brokerage households found the most active traders underperformed the market by **6.5% annually**, chiefly due to trading costs and buying stocks that subsequently underperform the ones sold ([Barber & Odean](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers%20current%20versions/individual_investor_performance_final.pdf)), and the SEC has brought real enforcement actions — including a ~$100 million pump-and-dump scheme and undisclosed-payment promotions by Kim Kardashian and Paul Pierce — against exactly the audience-monetization pattern critics describe ([SEC.gov](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2022-221)). Popularity, in this ecosystem, is not merely uncorrelated with accuracy — it runs in the opposite direction.

## Five priorities for Candor's next backtest

Given that insider-cluster-buying is the only one of five tested factors to show statistical significance, and that the broader literature's clearest, most specific actionable refinement sits exactly on top of that result, the highest-priority next step is **routine-versus-opportunistic insider-trade filtering**. Cohen, Malloy & Pomorski show that stripping out scheduled/routine Form 4 transactions (10b5-1 plans, predictable option-exercise patterns) isolates nearly all of the real signal, turning a diluted mixed sample into a cleaner one earning 82 basis points/month in their data ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16454/w16454.pdf)); this uses data Candor already has (EDGAR Form 4s), requires no new integration, and directly targets why the existing signal "wasn't yet trusted enough" — it is very plausibly being watered down by routine trades mixed into the cluster. Second, before trusting that refined signal (or the original one) further, Candor should run the **Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO) / combinatorially symmetric cross-validation** framework from Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu against its own testing process — this is a formal, free, implementable check on exactly the risk the user flagged ("not yet trusted enough to fully rely on"), and would turn a subjective judgment call into a quantified one ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2326253)). Third, add the **MAX effect / idiosyncratic volatility** factor: it is pure price-and-volume data already in Candor's Yahoo Finance pipeline, mechanically distinct from the momentum and low-volatility factors already tested (it targets lottery-like, high-skew stocks rather than trend or stability), and has a specific, well-cited magnitude (up to 1.03%/month spread) from a single-author-team academic source ([Bali, Cakici & Whitelaw](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~rwhitela/papers/max%20jfe11.pdf)). Fourth, test **net share issuance/buyback** derived from SEC EDGAR XBRL shares-outstanding tags: like insider trading, this is naturally point-in-time (the filing date is the information date), free, and conceptually distinct from the five factors already tried — though Candor should budget real engineering time for normalizing XBRL tags across filers, since this is the same data-wrangling cost that blocks value/quality testing more broadly. Fifth, before expanding the factor list further, apply a **liquidity floor** (minimum dollar volume and price) across both the existing and any new price-only factors; Hou, Xue & Zhang's finding that microcap stocks are ~60% of listed names but only ~3% of market cap, and disproportionately drive spurious "anomalies" that fail to replicate under proper weighting, is the single most transferable lesson from the institutional factor-zoo literature to a free-data retail context, and a dashboard with no fundamentals-based quality filter is structurally exposed to exactly this bias ([Replicating Anomalies](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/345507035_Replicating_Anomalies)). Notably absent from this list: sentiment, options flow, dark-pool signals, and raw-sequence deep learning — not because they are impossible to build, but because the credible evidence for each is either reverse-causal, paywalled at the data layer, or documented to fail honest out-of-sample testing, and pursuing them would trade a working methodology for a worse evidence base.

## Conclusion

The deepest pattern across all six approaches is that edge, where it exists at all, concentrates in signals that are structurally hard to arbitrage — because they require information not yet public (opportunistic insider trades before they're widely flagged), or because they sit in illiquid corners of the market that institutional capital finds uneconomical to exploit at scale. Everything popularized enough to reach retail audiences at scale — moving averages, Fibonacci levels, Twitter sentiment, unusual-options alerts, finfluencer calls — shows the same decay signature: real or plausible in a discovery sample, arbitraged or exposed as noise once published and widely adopted. Candor's own result (one significant factor out of five, and still not fully trusted) is not a disappointing outlier; it is close to what the academic record itself predicts for a careful, honest test. The more valuable move from here is not casting a wider net across new signal categories but tightening the lens on the one signal that already cleared the bar — refining it methodologically, stress-testing it for overfitting, and only then cautiously extending to one or two adjacent, similarly point-in-time-safe, free-data factors.
